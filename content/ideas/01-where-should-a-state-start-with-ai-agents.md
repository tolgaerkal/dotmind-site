# Where should a state start with AI agents?

*Tolga Erkal · October 27, 2026*

## The real question

Start where the work is high-volume, rule-based and already digital, not where the technology looks most impressive. That is the short answer, and it holds for almost every state.

Every state leader is being asked some version of “What’s our AI plan?” Vendors arrive with demos. Pilots appear in one agency and stall in the next. The pressure is to start somewhere visible.

But the useful question isn’t “where could AI agents go?” It’s “where will an agent reliably help residents and staff this year, with risks we can manage?” A recent framework from the World Economic Forum and Capgemini offers a practical way to answer it.

## Start with functions, not departments

The framework’s core idea is simple: plan around government functions, not departments or tools. A function is a recurring piece of work that cuts across agencies, like checking eligibility, validating documents or detecting fraud.

That matters because most states organize AI efforts by agency. The Department of Labor runs one pilot, Health and Human Services another, and neither learns from the other. But the work underneath is often identical. Checking a pay stub for an unemployment claim and checking one for a Medicaid renewal are the same job.

The report maps about 70 such functions across nine categories, from public services to policy planning. It then scores each one on two things, from one to four:

- **Potential:** how much an agent could improve the work.
- **Complexity:** how hard it would be to do safely, given the data, the stakes and how much human judgment is involved.

Readiness is potential minus complexity. High readiness means real value at manageable risk.

## Where agents are ready now, and where to wait

The highest readiness sits in recurring, rule-based work where data is already digital and mistakes can be caught. The lowest sits in policy work, where judgment and negotiation can’t be taken out.

The pattern is intuitive once you see it. Answering questions, booking appointments and checking documents follow clear rules, happen thousands of times a day, and leave a trail a person can review. Planning policy or reorganizing a workforce does not.

## What this means for state agencies

Three lessons stand out for states.

**The front door is the right first door.** Most of the high-readiness work sits where residents meet government: answering questions, booking appointments, checking documents and tracking cases. These are also where residents feel the most friction, so early wins are visible.

**Build once, use across agencies.** If a document-checking agent works for business licenses, most of it will work for benefit renewals. States that build around shared functions get a second service far faster than the first.

**Data and process come first.** The framework is clear that readiness depends on local conditions. If documents live in paper files, or if a process is unclear even to staff, an agent will automate confusion. Sometimes the first step is fixing the process, not adding AI.

## Five steps to pick your first agent

Here is the approach we use with agencies, adapted from the framework’s roadmap.

1. **List your high-volume interactions.** Applications, renewals, requests and reports that arrive by the thousand. Volume is where small gains add up.
2. **Find where people get stuck.** Look for resubmissions, backlogs and “where’s my application?” calls. Ask caseworkers, not just managers.
3. **Score each one honestly.** Rate potential and complexity from one to four. Be strict about data quality and about how much judgment the decision needs.
4. **Pick one with a clear measure.** Choose a high-readiness interaction where success is easy to see, such as fewer incomplete submissions or faster review times.
5. **Pilot with people in the loop.** Run the agent alongside the current process, with staff approving every decision. Measure, learn, then expand to the next service.

The goal of a first agent isn’t to transform government. It’s to prove, on real cases, that an agent can make one service better and safer, and to build the trust that lets the second one move faster.

## Start small, start where it counts

The states that get the most from AI agents won’t be the ones that start biggest. They’ll be the ones that start in the right place, prove value on real cases, and reuse what works.
